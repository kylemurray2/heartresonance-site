RR, ECG, or averaged BPM: choosing a heart rate sensor

"Heart rate sensor" covers at least three different kinds of data. A chest strap, a watch and an optical arm band can all show 62 on the screen and still hand an app very different material to work with. Here is what each kind is, what it's good for, what we saw when we wore a strap and a watch together, and which one we'd pick for this app.

Five kinds of heart data

Every heart rate device does the same first job: it detects heartbeats. What differs is how it detects them and how much of that detail it passes on. From most to least detailed:

KindWhat it isWhere it comes from
Raw ECG The heart's electrical waveform, sampled about 130 times a second on a Polar H10. Each beat has a recognisable shape. Electrodes on the skin of the chest.
RR intervals The time between one beat and the next, in milliseconds. A resting heart produces a sequence such as 987, 1052, 998, 1021. Computed on the strap from the ECG, then sent over Bluetooth.
Pulse intervals (PPI) The same idea measured from the pulse wave in the skin instead of the heart's electricity. Slightly softer timing. Optical sensors: arm bands, and the back of most watches. Few expose it over standard Bluetooth.
Averaged BPM One number, about once a second, usually a whole number, averaged over a window of recent beats. What most watches broadcast over Bluetooth.
Daily scores HRV status, recovery, readiness. A summary built from overnight data and a personal baseline. The watch's or ring's app, calculated once a day.

A detail that only matters if you write the software: the standard Bluetooth Heart Rate Profile carries each RR interval as a whole number of 1/1024-second steps, so a strap that supports it reports beat timing in steps of about a millisecond. A BPM-only sensor never sends that field at all. The app receives a single integer and has to work from it.

The earlier post on what HRV actually measures explains why the daily scores answer a different question from a live session. This post is about the middle three rows, because those are what an app sees during a breathing session.

Which kind is good for what

None of these is better in general. Each is the right shape for some jobs and the wrong shape for others, and most of the confusion comes from using one for the other's job.

If you want to…You needWhy
Gauge effort during a run or ride Averaged BPM A steady, readable number is the point. Smoothing helps here.
Follow resting heart rate or overnight trends Daily scores Built for exactly this, and comfortable to wear all night.
See your heart rate rise and fall with a slow breath RR intervals, or BPM at slow paces The wave is large when you breathe slowly, so even an averaged signal shows it.
Compare breathing paces, or find which pace gives the cleanest wave RR intervals for the finest detail; averaged BPM for a good estimate Faster paces make a smaller wave, and averaging flattens small waves first, so differences between paces are easier to see with RR intervals.
Read an HRV number in milliseconds (RMSSD, SDNN) RR intervals These are statistics on beat-to-beat differences. An averaged signal has no such differences left.
Check for skipped or extra beats, or validate another sensor ECG Only the waveform shows what each beat looked like.

What averaging does to the breathing wave

Averaging is a good design choice for a watch. A watch that showed every beat's raw timing would be jumpy and hard to read in the middle of a workout, and a steady number is what you want there. For breathing work it has three predictable effects worth knowing about.

  • It trims fast rhythms more than slow ones. Any average over a few seconds barely touches a 14-second cycle and removes more of an 8-second one. Whatever filter a particular watch uses, this is how averaging behaves.
  • It runs late. An average is built from beats that already happened, so the displayed value sits behind the heart.
  • Whole numbers add a staircase. One bpm is a large step when the wave you want to see is only a few bpm tall.

Slow breathing is the friendliest case for an averaged signal, because the wave you're producing is slow and large. The effects grow as the wave gets smaller and faster.

What we measured: a strap and a watch, worn together

We wore a Polar H10 and a Garmin Forerunner 165 (with Broadcast Heart Rate on) for a 35-minute session: normal breathing, a full assessment, five minutes near the pace the assessment chose, five minutes at a faster pace, and a few minutes of talking and moving. A Mac recorded both sensors on one clock, along with the H10's raw ECG. The app ran on the strap at the same time.

Two minutes at 4.3 breaths per minute, from the recording. Each breath is one rise and fall. The strap's wave is taller and sharper; the watch's is smaller, stepped, and arrives a few seconds later, which is what averaging does.

The strap held up against its own ECG. Over 1,959 heartbeats, the intervals the H10 reported differed from intervals measured on its own ECG by a median of 2.4 ms. That is why we treated it as the reference for everything else.

The watch followed the same rhythm. It's a smaller copy, as expected from an averaged signal. Here is how much of the strap's breathing wave the watch trace contained at each pace:

Breathing paceWatch wave as a share of the strap's
4.0–4.5 breaths/minabout 43%
4.5–5.0about 30%
5.0–5.5about 20%
5.5–6.5about 15–17%
6.5 and fasterabout 12–15%

That matches the three effects above: the slower the breath, the more of the wave comes through. The watch's reading also arrives about 7 seconds after the strap's, which is normal for an averaged value (the strap itself reports each beat about 2 seconds after it happens, which we confirmed against the ECG). Delay doesn't change the shape of the wave, but it is worth knowing about when you compare the live graph to the pacer.

The watch gave a good estimate where it counts. It scored the slower hold higher than the faster one, as the strap did. And when we took the assessment the app ran on the strap and re-scored the same six paces from the watch's data, it picked the same winner, 4.0 breaths per minute. Both sources dipped at 5.5 and peaked at 4.0.

What this means for the assessment

A watch gives a decent estimate of your pace. The strap scored the six paces between 0.73 and 0.85, with 4.0 on top. The watch scored them from 0.23 to 0.72, with the same pace on top and a wider spread, since averaging lowers every score somewhat. One thing we haven't tested: 4.0 was the slowest pace in the assessment, where a watch's signal is strongest. We haven't yet recorded someone whose best pace is near 6, so for a result you want to be sure of, a strap is a good way to confirm it.

Millisecond HRV is where a strap adds the most. RMSSD over the same session was about 32 ms from the strap and about 10 ms from the watch's broadcast, because averaging removes most of the beat-to-beat differences that RMSSD is made of. A watch is fine for the live wave; for an RMSSD number in milliseconds, use a strap.

Limits: this is one person, one sitting, and one watch model. Other watches smooth differently, and a watch's own app may have access to more than its Bluetooth broadcast does. Treat the numbers as a measured example, not a rating of any brand.

Our recommendation: a Polar H10

Disclosure

We are not affiliated with Polar, Garmin, Wahoo, Apple or any other sensor brand, and we receive no sponsorship, payment, free products or commission from any of them. This page has no affiliate links. The strap and watch we tested are our own, and the recommendation is based on what we measured.

For the most detailed picture, finding your resonance pace and watching the wave closely, we'd pick a Polar H10 chest strap. The reasons come straight from the sections above.

  • It sends real RR intervals. Every number the app computes, from the wave on the live graph to RMSSD, comes from actual beat timing, not a reconstruction.
  • Its timing is verified. Our own comparison with its ECG found a median gap of 2.4 ms, and published validation studies against clinical ECG reach the same conclusion.
  • It needs no setup. It uses the standard Bluetooth Heart Rate Profile and starts broadcasting when worn. There is no menu toggle, no bridge app and no foreground app to keep open.
  • It accepts two Bluetooth connections at once, so it can feed this app and another device together. We used that for the recording above.

The cost is that you wear a strap: moisten the electrodes, put it on a minute or two before you start so the signal settles, and keep it somewhere that dries. Other dedicated chest straps that report RR intervals, such as the Garmin HRM-Pro or HRM-Dual and the Wahoo TICKR, serve the same purpose. The device guide lists them and how to pair each one.

And if you have a watch

A watch works well with the app. It's already on your wrist, it needs no new purchase, and slow breathing produces a wave large enough for it to show clearly. Use it for the assessment, for practice sessions, and for the all-day and overnight tracking that straps aren't meant for. The assessment gives you a decent estimate of your pace. A strap is the upgrade for finer detail: telling close paces apart, confirming your result, and reading HRV in milliseconds. You don't have to choose one for good. Many people keep both.

In the app

The app uses real RR intervals whenever the sensor sends them and falls back to the BPM stream when it doesn't. The assessment works with either; its intro page mentions that a chest strap gives the most accurate result.
